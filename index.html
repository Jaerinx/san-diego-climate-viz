San Diego Climate Over the Last Century

Five static exploratory charts built with D3.js: NOAA CMIP6 historical simulations (GFDL-ESM4) for temperature, precipitation, and sea level near San Diego, plus U.S. Census county population density (1925–2014).

1. Warming trend in annual mean temperature

Year-to-year variability with a 5-year moving average reveals gradual warming near San Diego over the last nine decades of the historical run.

2. Temperature anomalies from a 1961–1990 baseline

Bars show departure from the late-20th-century climatological mean — a standard reference for detecting recent warming.

3. Modeled sea level near the San Diego coast

CMIP6 sea surface height (zos) at the nearest ocean grid cell shows long-term variability and rise relative to a 1961–1990 baseline (millimeters).

4. Year-to-year precipitation totals

Modeled annual rainfall shows Mediterranean-style wet winters and dry summers aggregated to yearly totals; dashed line marks the 1961–1990 mean.

5. San Diego County population density

U.S. Census decennial counts (red dots) define county population; density uses a fixed land area of 4,207 sq mi with linear interpolation between census years.

Write-Up

So far, we have built a prototype for our interactive charts called "San Diego Climate Over the Last Century" with the 5 D3.js visualizations. We puled NOAA CMIP6 historical simulation data to report things like annual mean temperature, temperature anomalies, yearly precipitation, and sea surface height near SD. We also added the U.S. Census county population density from 1925-2014 to provide more context for our climate data. We wrote explanations for each visualization but kept the layout simple for readability and a story-based webpage so that the reader learns in order as they scroll through the page - learning about warming, anomalies, sea level, rain, then population in order.

The most challenging part of our project to design will likely be combining the information from the five separate plots into one clear narrative. Currently, each visualization stands on its own and mostly has its own story, but moving forward we will need to balance text and interaction in a way that avoids overplotting. Also, it will be challenging but necessary to present sea level and precipitation information without misleading readers, since the data comes from CMIP6 model simulations, not actual measurements. We will also need to combine and cleanly align population density and the climate series since they come from different sources. However, this is definitely worthy of our time as it will provide another layer of context of what climate changes actually mean for the population.

Data: NOAA Public CMIP6 on Google Cloud. Course context: DSC 106 Project 3. CMIP6 values are model simulations. Population data are from the U.S. Census, not CMIP6.
